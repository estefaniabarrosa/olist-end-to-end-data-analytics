Olist doesn't have an acquisition problem. It has a delivery problem wearing an acquisition costume.

I started this analysis expecting seller acquisition — or price — to explain customer satisfaction. Neither did. What explained it was whether the package arrived when the customer was promised it would.

The question

Three levers. Limited budget. Which one actually moves?

Olist is a Brazilian marketplace that lets small businesses sell online without building their own e-commerce infrastructure. Around 3,000 sellers, customers across the country, and growth that depends on three things working together.

The tempting answer is to fund all three. The useful answer is to know which one the evidence actually supports. So I tested each against the outcome it is supposed to move.

Finding 01 — Delivery and satisfaction

On time: 4.29 stars. Late: 2.57.

That gap of 1.7 stars is the single widest split anywhere in the dataset — wider than any product category, and far wider than anything price explains. A Welch's t-test across 88,163 on-time and 7,661 late orders returns t = 89.4, p < 0.001.

Product category and price also test as significant. At almost 100,000 orders, most things do. What separates delivery is the size of the difference, not the p-value: the median order value barely shifts between a one-star and a five-star review, while delivery moves the average rating by nearly two full stars.

Finding 02 — Where the problem lives

The worst rate is not where you start.

Late-delivery rates range from 5.6% in Minas Gerais to 23.9% in Alagoas, against a national average of 8.1%. A single national delivery policy hides all of that.

Finding 03 — The result that changed my mind

Acquisition channel told us nothing.

Descriptively, organic search looked stronger than paid search — R$1,832 average seller GMV against R$1,537. That is the kind of gap a marketing team would happily build a budget around.

It doesn't survive testing. Across the four main channels the difference sits inside normal variation, and it stays there when the test is repeated without assuming a normal distribution.

Shapiro-Wilk rejected normality for all four channels, so the ANOVA was repeated non-parametrically. Both tests agree, which means the conclusion doesn't depend on which one you trust. A null result is still a result: it tells the business where not to spend on the strength of an assumption.

From analysis to action

What I'd put in front of the CEO.

  1. Fix delivery reliability in Rio de Janeiro first

    One state, a fifth of every late delivery in the marketplace. It is the only place where a bad rate and real volume overlap at scale.

  2. Manage logistics regionally, not as a national average

    An 8.1% national figure conceals a four-fold spread. Track the late rate by state weekly so problems surface before they reach reviews.

  3. Stop treating acquisition channel as a quality signal

    It doesn't predict seller value in this data. Post-onboarding performance does, and it's already being measured.

  4. Watch the promise, not just the delivery

    On-time rate can be "improved" by quoting later dates. Any target on delivery reliability needs the estimated dates monitored alongside it, or the metric moves without the customer noticing anything.

Honest scope

What this analysis does not prove.

Every number above has a boundary. These are the four that would change how much weight I'd put on the conclusions.

Survivorship

Of 842 acquired sellers, only 380 appear selling in the marketplace data. The acquisition comparison is conditioned on having made at least one sale, so a channel that loses sellers before their first order would be invisible here.

Association, not cause

Delivery dates come from the order table rather than from the review, so lateness isn't self-reported by unhappy customers. But late deliveries may still travel with other problems this data can't separate.

Attribution

Order-level data cannot tell whether a delay originated with the seller, the carrier, or Olist. That matters, because the three imply completely different interventions.

Recency

The data covers 2016–2018. The mechanism — that missing a promised date drives one-star reviews — is structural. The specific state rates would need refreshing.

How it was built

Python to SQL to Tableau, cross-checked at each handoff.

Two public Olist datasets joined on seller_id: the e-commerce data and the seller acquisition funnel. Core business metrics were reproduced in SQL and compared against the pandas results, because a join that silently duplicates rows is the failure mode that survives every visual check.

Clean & validate

Python, pandas, numpy. Nulls, duplicates, type casting, and relational integrity across nine tables.

Test

scipy. Shapiro-Wilk, one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis, Welch's t-test. H₀/H₁ and α stated for each.

Reproduce in SQL

MySQL via SQLAlchemy. CTEs and window functions — RANK() OVER, SUM() OVER — for the state prioritisation.

Communicate

A seven-sheet Tableau dashboard with cross-filtering, and a twelve-slide deck for a CEO audience.
